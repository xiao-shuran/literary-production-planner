# Narration Guide

Use this guide only when the plan uses narration-led or hybrid mode.

## Purpose Of Narration

Narration should do work that staging and dialogue cannot do economically: establish essential time or place, compress events over a long span, suggest interior change that cannot be externalized, bridge scenes, or sustain the work's central imagery. It is neither a plot summary nor a frame-by-frame description of visible action.

A strong narration passage usually has one clear job and leads naturally into a concrete action, image, silence, or line of dialogue. Give it a readable rhythm: vary sentence length, keep information density manageable, and land cleanly. Use language that suits the source's tone without ornamental excess.

## Writing Rules

- Identify what the scene already shows, then add only the critical information the audience cannot learn from the performance.
- Prefer concrete time, feeling, relationship change, and imagery to abstract words such as `fate`, `redemption`, or `impact` without supporting action.
- Do not give away dialogue a character is about to say, or explain the same point again after the dialogue.
- Keep the narrator's viewpoint and voice stable. If switching to a character's interior monologue, label the switch and keep its boundary clear.
- At a sensitive turn in the plot, be concise and let the following performance carry the conflict; do not make narration perform it for the actors.
- If the user requests no narration, do not use this guide or substitute `voice-over`, `host links`, or another form of narration.

## Self-Check

- Would the audience lose necessary information if this line were removed? If not, delete it or replace it with action or dialogue.
- Can one narrator say it comfortably while leaving performance space for the next scene?
- Does the language serve the source and scene rather than display literary flourish?
