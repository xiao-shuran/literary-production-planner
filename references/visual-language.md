# Visual Language Guide

Use this guide for video, film, storyboard, and other visually led adaptations. Use the applicable parts for stage work when repeated props, lighting, projection, or physical action create its expressive logic.

## Visual Motif System

A visual motif is a concrete, recurring element whose meaning develops as the story progresses. It is not a logo, a theme statement, or a collection of unrelated attractive shots.

Choose one to three motifs from material already present in the work: a prop, repeated gesture, place, color contrast, sound, weather condition, light source, threshold, or spatial relationship. For every motif, specify:

| Field | Decision to make |
| --- | --- |
| Concrete carrier | What the audience repeatedly sees or hears. |
| Initial meaning | What it signifies when first introduced. |
| Development | How context, framing, condition, or use changes at later appearances. |
| Final meaning | What the final return lets the audience understand or feel. |
| Execution rule | How camera, sound, color, staging, or editing keeps the recurrence legible without overemphasizing it. |

Use a motif return only when its context has changed or it can create a deliberate resonance. Do not insert the same insert shot at fixed intervals just to prove that a motif exists. In a short piece, two meaningful appearances may be sufficient.

## Emotional Rhythm Curve

Define emotional rhythm as a sequence of changing audience states, not an instruction to make each later scene louder or sadder. Before drafting detailed shots, make a compact beat map with:

| Beat | Audience state | Dramatic function | Film-language choice |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opening condition | What the audience should feel first | Establish place, distance, or expectation | Tempo, framing, ambient sound, color |
| First turn | What changes in the relationship or situation | Create engagement or friction | Change in shot size, movement, cut pace, or silence |
| Pressure / deepening | What grows more difficult, intimate, or uncertain | Accumulate stakes | Repetition with variation, compression, visual contrast |
| Peak | The strongest irreversible recognition, decision, loss, or connection | Deliver the central emotional turn | Simplify the image; protect performance, sound, or silence |
| Release / residue | What remains after the peak | Let the theme settle without explaining it away | Longer hold, return of a motif, quieter sound, visual space |

Adapt the map to the actual source. Comedies may peak through release; mysteries may withhold until late; quiet literary works may use a small internal shift rather than a loud climax. Do not confuse constant intensity with rhythm. Contrast is what makes intensity readable.

## Shot Value Judgment

For every shot in a storyboard, name its primary value in the “purpose” or “shot value” field. A shot should normally have one primary value and no more than one secondary value:

- `Story`: supplies new causal information or advances the event.
- `Character`: reveals an inner state, decision, or changed behavior.
- `Relationship`: changes or clarifies the distance, power, care, or conflict between people.
- `Orientation`: establishes place, time, direction, or a necessary action connection.
- `Motif`: develops the chosen recurring visual or sound element.
- `Rhythm`: creates contrast, pause, acceleration, anticipation, or release.
- `Atmosphere`: establishes a world or feeling the story requires and cannot get more economically elsewhere.

Ask these tests in order:

1. What does the audience know or feel after this shot that it did not know or feel before?
2. Could the adjacent shot carry that job without reducing clarity, emotion, or rhythm?
3. Does the shot repeat information, gesture, framing, or music without a changed meaning?
4. Does its duration match its value? Let performance and realization breathe; cut redundant setup and illustrative inserts.

If a shot fails these tests, cut it, combine it with its neighbor, or give it a clearer job. “It looks cinematic” is not enough by itself.

## Practical Integration

When the user asks for a full planning document, integrate this work inside the requested structure:

- In “creative core” or “visual style,” give a concise motif system.
- In “structure” or “sequence design,” show the emotional beat map.
- In the storyboard, add a `Shot value` / `镜头价值` column when the user's format allows it. When it does not, include the value in the visual-description cell.
- In staging plans, replace “shot value” with “stage image value” and assess the same functions.

State only decisions that change the actual plan. Avoid arbitrary color symbolism, ornamental slow motion, hand-held movement without motivation, or music cues that tell the audience exactly what to feel.
