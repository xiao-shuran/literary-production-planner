# Literary Production Planner

Turn literary material into a plan that can be rehearsed, filmed, recorded, or performed. The user owns the planning document's structure; this skill organizes the source material, presentation design, character performance, and optional narration into an executable plan.

## Establish The Brief

Gather available information before making assumptions about a competition, school, cast size, or planning-document format. Key inputs include:

- The source text, a reliable synopsis, an excerpt, or the work's title; plus the theme, plot, characters, and ending the user wants retained.
- Purpose and medium: stage, short video, audio, recital, classroom presentation, competition, or another form; including duration, cast size, venue, budget, technical limits, and audience.
- Cast configuration and naming convention, such as character names or labels such as `Female Lead 1` and `Male Lead 2`.
- Requirements for language, period setting, adaptation scope, dialogue style, and output language.
- **Planning-document structure:** the user's headings, order, word limits, and table format take priority. Ask for an empty template or example headings when useful.
- Whether narration is needed. If unspecified, decide under “Choose A Script Mode” and identify the choice in the deliverable.

When missing information would risk changing the work's core meaning, ask concise, specific questions first. If the user asks for a direct draft, make the most conservative reasonable assumptions and group them at the start of the deliverable. If no structure is provided, propose an editable set of headings for approval; never treat it as a mandatory template.

## Diagnose Before You Template

The planning-document template is a container, not an artistic prescription. Do not make a poem, a quiet essay, a mystery, a historical record, and a relationship-driven novel resemble the same short film merely because they use the same headings.

Before outlining scenes, create a brief internal **adaptation diagnosis**. Identify:

- `Source form`: short story, novel excerpt, drama, essay/prose, poem/lyrics, letters/diary, historical or documentary material, myth/fable, or mixed form.
- `Narrative engine`: event and causality, relationship and conflict, psychological interiority, atmosphere and imagery, ideas and argument, mystery and revelation, testimony and memory, ritual or allegory.
- `Audience contract`: what a faithful audience most needs to experience: plot clarity, character pressure, a distinct voice, mood, a question, factual trust, surprise, or an after-image.
- `Adaptation pressure`: time span, cast limits, location limits, need for narration, rights/source limits, and intended medium.
- `Primary form`: the delivery form best suited to this source and brief: dialogue scene, narrated image poem, documentary-style montage, staged reading, ensemble performance, interview/testimony format, suspense sequence, or another justified form.

State the diagnosis concisely when it would help the user understand a non-obvious structural choice. If the user has supplied a fixed template, preserve all its headings, but make the content under each heading answer the diagnosed work rather than copy a previous project's proportions, scene count, narration density, or visual style.

Read the [narrative routing guide](references/narrative-routing.md) for every new literary adaptation. It contains routing choices and failure tests for distinct source types.

## Choose A Script Mode

Select a mode based on the presentation need. Do not make narration a default component of every plan:

- **Dialogue-led (no narration):** Use when relationships, conflict, and turns can be conveyed naturally through performable action, scenes, and character dialogue. Label dialogue by character name or cast code, with only necessary staging, sound, and transition cues.
- **Narration-led:** Use when essential context must be supplied quickly, or the work depends on interiority, long time spans, a complex viewpoint, or background that is hard to stage. Narration bridges events and establishes atmosphere; characters should still act and speak in pivotal conflicts.
- **Hybrid:** Use when brief context, time compression, or scene links are needed but dramatic conflict should remain with the characters. This is often suitable for adapting prose, novels, or multi-thread narratives to a short runtime.

When the user explicitly requests no narration, do not reintroduce it as voice-over, host links, or renamed explanatory text. For narration-led or hybrid work, read the [narration guide](references/narration.md). For medium-specific production design, read the [presentation and rehearsal guide](references/production-design.md).

## Build The Expressive System

For visual or audiovisual adaptations, do not move straight from plot summary to a shot list. First decide what recurring visual and sound elements make the work feel like one coherent film.

- Identify one to three **visual motifs** drawn from the source's objects, actions, spaces, colors, weather, light, or repeated gestures. A motif must change meaning across the story; it is not decoration.
- Map the **emotional rhythm** across scenes: establish the audience's starting state, introduce pressure or desire, create contrast and release, locate the emotional high point, and leave a deliberate after-feeling. Match scene duration, shot scale, movement, sound, and silence to this curve.
- Make a **shot-value judgment** for every planned shot. A shot must add new story information, reveal a relationship or inner change, establish spatial/time orientation, build motif, alter rhythm, or create a necessary emotional beat. Combine or cut shots that only repeat an already-understood idea.
- Keep the system proportionate to the work. A short, simple piece may only need one motif and a five-beat rhythm; a longer film may need a motif map and sequence-level rhythm plans. Do not force symbolism into a realist work that does not support it.

Read the [visual language guide](references/visual-language.md) for all video, film, or storyboard plans. Read it for stage work when lighting, props, projection, or repeated physical action carry a central motif. Before submitting an audiovisual plan or reviewing a finished cut, read the [final quality review guide](references/final-quality-review.md).

## Adaptation Workflow

1. Diagnose the work's source form, narrative engine, audience contract, adaptation pressure, and primary form. Route the adaptation accordingly before choosing scenes, narration density, or visual language.
2. Extract the source material's non-negotiable elements. The list will differ by type: a plot-driven work may require causal turns; a poem may require voice, imagery, and movement; a historical piece may require factual provenance and ethical framing. Separate source facts from production-driven compression or merging.
3. Select a small number of scenes, images, voices, or units that carry the diagnosed engine within the target duration. Minor characters or events may be merged, but do not silently reverse motivation, moral position, factual trust, or ending. Label substantive departures as `Adaptation choice`.
4. For video or other visual media, establish the visual motif system and scene-by-scene emotional rhythm before writing the shot list. Record them under the user's existing headings, or add a compact “Visual design” / “Rhythm” note only when their structure permits it.
5. Design every scene as an executable unit: purpose, participating roles, location/time, action or image, dialogue or narration, transition, sound/light/prop needs, and estimated duration. Include only the details that fit the user's requested structure.
6. Give each shot or stage image an explicit value. Retain it only if it performs a distinct narrative, relational, spatial, rhythmic, motif, or emotional function; otherwise merge it, replace it, or cut it.
7. Make dialogue speakable, performable, and capable of changing a relationship or situation. Do not merely convert prose sentences into character speech, and do not make every character sound alike.
8. Complete the plan in the user's required headings and order. If a heading is abstract or lacks essential implementation information, add the needed detail inside that heading or flag the gap briefly before delivery; do not rearrange the entire plan unilaterally.

## Output Requirements

- Use the user's requested language, headings, table format, and role labels. If unspecified, use the language of the user's request.
- Clearly distinguish planning notes, scenes/shots, character dialogue, narration, and staging cues; do not blend them into an ambiguous block.
- Do not invent uncertain source facts. With no source text or reliable synopsis, flag facts requiring verification and request material from the user.
- For copyrighted modern works, work primarily from user-provided excerpts, a synopsis, or original adaptation ideas. Do not claim access to the full work or reproduce unprovided long passages.
- Unless requested, avoid generic slogans, irrelevant background, and non-actionable suggestions. Every retained section should help planning, rehearsal, filming, or evaluation.

## Pre-Delivery Check

- Does each required source element have a corresponding scene, dialogue passage, or narration passage?
- Does the chosen primary form fit this particular work's narrative engine, rather than reproducing a generic or previous-project format?
- Does the use of scenes, narration, dialogue, images, factual material, and performance preserve the audience contract of this source type?
- Does the script mode match the user's request, without accidentally adding or omitting narration?
- Are cast size, role codes, entrances, dialogue attribution, and plot causality consistent?
- Is narration speakable, complementary to the action, and free of repetition of what actors already perform?
- Are duration, venue, props, and technical choices feasible?
- Does the result strictly follow the user's chosen planning structure?
- For audiovisual work, does the plan identify an intentional motif system, emotional rhythm, and a distinct value for every retained shot?
- Has the plan or finished work passed the relevant checks in the final quality review guide, with concrete revisions made for material failures?
